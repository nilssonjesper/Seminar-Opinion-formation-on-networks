\begin{abstract}
    Understanding how opinions emerge and evolve within a population is central to explaining collective behavior and social dynamics. In this review paper, we take a closer look at the DeGroot model (1974) and analyze its convergence and network properties. We show that if the weight matrix $\mat A$ is primitive, then the model converges to a consensus. We also prove that this condition can be verified by checking whether the corresponding graph is strongly connected and aperiodic. Moreover, we show that the connectedness of the graph provides an upper and lower bound for the second largest eigenvalue of $\mat A$, which in turn gives us an upper bound for the convergence speed of the model. Finally, we take a look at the Friedkin-Johnsen model (1990, 1999) and a bounded-confidence model. For the former, we show that the model converges if the product $\mat D\mat A$ of the \textit{susceptibility} matrix $\mat D$ and $\mat A$ is irreducible. For the latter, we provide a procedure to ensure the convergence of the model.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
As individuals interact and exchange views, local patterns of agreement or disagreement can propagate through social networks, leading to large-scale outcomes such as consensus, polarization, or fragmentation. The question of how, for example, echo chambers form and influence opinions is a strongly debated topic in our society.

In order to better understand these phenomena, many attempts have been made to analyze and model the formation of opinions, like the voter \cite{Voter-model} and Ising model \cite{Ising-model}, as well as the Friedkin and Johnsen model (1990, \cite{Friedkin01011990}). Another well-known example is the DeGroot Model (1974, \cite{Degroot01031974}) which presents a rather simple, iterative process.
Given an instance of this model, we can then analyze or calculate properties such as whether the group will reach a consensus (see in \cref{sec:when_convergence} and \cref{sec:reaching_consensus}), what the consensus they reach is(see in \cref{sec:calculating_conv}), and how fast they will reach it (see in \cref{sec:speed_convergence}). Moreover, the model can be represented and visualized as a graph with the nodes and edges representing who is communicating with whom. Thereby, we can use features of these graphs as conditions and explanations for the aforementioned properties (see in \cref{sec:network_prop}).

As the DeGroot model is a rather simple abstraction of opinion formation in real social networks, it does not capture all aspects of social interaction. Therefore, we will take a look at two extensions of the DeGroot Model (see in \cref{sec:extensions}). First, the aforementioned Friedkin-Johnsen model and second, the Hegselmann-Krause model.

\section{Preliminaries and Notation}\label{sec:prelim_notation}
In this paper, we use $\ones$ to denote the vector with all-ones and $\eye$ to denote the identity matrix. Similarly, the vector with all-zeros is referred to as $\zeros$. We use the following style for vectors $\vec p$, matrices $\mat M$, and graphs $\gG$. Moreover, $V(\gG)$ denotes the set of vertices and $E(\gG)$ the set of edges in $\gG$. For a matrix $\mat M$, the notation $(M)_{ij}$ refers to the entry in the i$th$ row and j$th$ column of $\mat M$. Furthermore, we use the notation $\underline{n} = \{1,\dots,n\}$. 

A row-stochastic matrix $\mat A \in \{0,1\}^{n\times n}$ is defined, such that for all its entries $a_{ij} \geq 0$ and its row vectors sum up to 1, i.e. $\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij} = 1$ for all $i \in \underline{n}$. A row-substochastic matrix $\mat A \in \{0,1\}^{n\times n}$ is a non-negative matrix with at least one row vector that sums up to less than 1, i.e. $\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij} \leq 1$ for all $j\in \underline{n}$ and $\sum_{j=1}^na_{ij} < 1$ for at least one $j\in \underline{n}$. Furthermore, a digraph describes a directed graph, meaning every edge has a direction. A graph is strongly connected if and only if every node $v \in V(\gG)$ is reachable from every other node $u \in V(\gG)$ (with $v \neq u$), i.e. there exists a directed path from every node $u$ to $v$. A sink $\gS$ is a strongly connected subgraph, i.e. $V(\gS) \subset V(\gG)$ and $E(\gS) \subset E(\gG)$, that has no node $i\in \gS$, such that there exists a node $j\notin \gS$ with $(i,j)\in E(\gG)$.
Lastly, a graph is aperiodic if and only if there exists no $k \in \NN_{>1}$, that divides the length of every simple cycle of the graph. A simple cycle refers to every possible path from any node $v$ back to itself.

\section{DeGroot model}\label{sec:degroot}
The DeGroot model considers a group of $n$ individuals who discuss their own opinions on a given parameter $\theta$, that describes the subject, with one another. Each of these individuals has their own opinion value for this unknown parameter $\theta$ which we will refer to as $v_i$ for $i \in \underline{n}$. The model makes use of the idea that interaction with other people in the group, can cause an individual $i$ to change their opinion value $v_i$ based on new information or expertise provided by the other individuals.


The model assumes that this revision of the individual's opinion is a linear combination of the opinion values $v_1, \dots, v_n$. To model this revision, we therefore define $a_{ij}$ which denotes the weight that individual $i$ assigns to the opinion of individual $j$. If, for example, individual $j$ is a leading expert on the value of $\theta$, then individual $i$ might want to assign a greater weight to individual $j$'s opinion. Likewise, individual $i$ can also assign a large weight to their own opinion and a small total weight to the opinions of other individuals. Another possible approach would be to weigh those opinions highly which align the most with one's own opinion. The weights are assigned by the individuals without any knowledge of the other individuals' assigned weights. 

Let $v_{ik}$ denote the opinion value of individual $i$ after revision $k$, then a revised opinion value of the individual $i$ is calculated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:opinion_revision}
    v_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij} v_{j(k-1)}\ .
\end{equation}
It is assumed that $a_{ij} \geq 0$ and $\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij} = 1$, meaning the weights assigned by a each individual sum up to 1, therefore the matrix representing the weights
\[ A = \begin{bmatrix}
a_{11} & \dots & a_{1n}\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
a_{n1} & \dots & a_{nn}
\end{bmatrix}, \]
is row-stochastic, as all its entries are non-negative and its rows have unit sum.


If we let the vector $\vec{p_0} = (v_1,\dots,v_n)^\T$ denote the initial opinion values and $\vec{p_k}=(v_{1k},\dots,v_{nk})^\T$ denote the opinion values after $k$ revisions, we can express a given revision as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iteration}
    \vec{p_k} = \mat A\vec{p_{k-1}} = \mat A^k\vec{p_0} \ ,
\end{equation}
for $k = 1, 2, \dots$\textbf{.} This process of revising the opinions is then repeated until the vector $\vec p_k$ converges asymptotically, with 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convergence}
    \vec{p_k} = \vec{p_{k+1}} = \mat A\vec{p_k}\ .
\end{equation}
As $\mat A\vec{p_k} = \mat A^k\vec{p_0}$, see \cref{eq:iteration}, it follows that 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:convergence_matrix}
    \mat A^k\mat A\vec{p_0} = \mat A^k\vec{p_0}\ ,
\end{equation}
i.e. $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k$ converges. We will refer to this converged matrix as $\mat{A^{fin}}$ and to the converged final opinion values as $\vec{p^{fin}} = (v_1^{fin},\dots,v_n^{fin})$. \cite{Degroot01031974}

If the row-stochastic matrix $\mat M$, shown on the left in \cref{fig:non_convergence_heatmap}, is multiplied by itself, it takes the form that is shown on the left for uneven powers $\mat M^{2k+1}$, and the form on the right for even powers $\mat M^{2k}$. Every square matrix $\mat A \in \RR^{n\times n}$ is similar to a block diagonal matrix $\mat J \in \RR^{n\times n}$, also called the Jordan normal form of $\mat A$, meaning $\mat A = \mat T \mat J \mat T^{-1}$ for an invertible matrix $\mat T \in \RR^{n\times n}$. This Jordan matrix consists of so-called Jordan blocks, where each block $\mat J_i$ has the form
\[\mat J_i^k = \begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_i & 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_i & \ddots  & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 1 & 0 \\
0 & \ddots & 0 & \lambda_i & 1 \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & \lambda_i
\end{bmatrix}\ , \]
where $\lambda_i$ is the i$th$ eigenvalue of $\mat A$. The size of a Jordan block $J_i$ is equal to the dimension of the eigenspace of the corresponding eigenvalue.

As $\mat A^k = \mat T \mat J^k \mat T^{-1}$, %begründen/zeigen mit TJT-1T,... (t-1 & t kürzen sich)
we can use this equation to find the limit of $\mat A^k$, as $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k = \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty} \mat T \mat J^k \mat T^{-1}$. Therefore, it suffices to show that $\mat J^k$ converges, to prove that $\mat A^k$ converges.

As the k$th$ power of a Jordan block $\mat J_i \in \RR^{l\times l}$ with the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ has the form 
\[\mat J_i^k = \begin{bmatrix}
\lambda_i^k & \binom{k}{1}\lambda_i^{k-1} & \binom{k}{2}\lambda_i^{k-2} & \dots & \binom{k}{l-1}\lambda_i^{k-l+1}\\
0 & \lambda_i^k & \binom{k}{1}\lambda_i^{k-1} & \ddots & \vdots\\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \binom{k}{2}\lambda_i^{k-2}\\
0 & \ddots & 0 & \lambda_i^k & \binom{k}{1}\lambda_i^{k-1}\\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & \lambda_i^k
\end{bmatrix}\ , \] % ref bullo
see \cite{Bullo_lecture},
we can conclude the following conditions for the convergence of matrix $\mat A$. First, all of its eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ need to be in the intervall $ -1 < |\lambda_i| \leq 1$. If $|\lambda_i| > 1$ then all non-zero entries of $\mat{J_i}$ diverge for $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mat{J_i}^k)_{ij} = \infty$. When some Jordan block $J_i$ diverges for $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}$ then trivially the Jordan matrix and thus $\mat A$ diverge as well.
Moreover, if $\lambda_j = -1$ is an eigenvalue of $\mat A$, then the entries $\lambda_j$ on the diagonal of the corresponding Jordan block $\mat{J_j}$ diverge for $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mat{J_i}^k)_{ij}$, as the powers $\lambda_j^k$ oscillate between $-1$ and $1$. Thus, $\mat A$ oscillates as well, as can be seen in \cref{fig:non_convergence_heatmap}.
Second, if $1$ is an eigenvalue of $\mat A$, it needs to be semisimple, meaning that its geometric and algebraic multiplicities are equal, i.e. the corresponding Jordan block $\mat J_1$ has size $1$.
If this condition is not satisfied, the entries $(J)_{ij}$ with $j>i$ diverge for $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mat{J_i}^k)_{ij} = \infty$. In the following, we will refer to the eigenvalue 1 of $\mat A$, also called the dominant eigenvalue, as $\lambda_1$.
Moreover, because $T$ is invertible, we know that $\mat T,\mat T^{-1}\neq \zerosmatr$. Therefore, if $\mat A$ has the semisimple eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, then 
\[\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat{A}^k = \mat T(\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat{J}^k)\mat T^{-1} \neq \zerosmatr \ ,\] as the singular value in the Jordan block $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}{J_1}$ converges against 1, i.e. $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}J^k \neq \zerosmatr$. We refer to such a matrix $\mat A$ as semi-convergent. 
% On the contrary, if $\mat A$ has no eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, then $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k = \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat J^k = \zerosmatr$ and is referred to as convergent.
Because $\mat A$ is row-stochastic, by definition
\begin{equation}%\label{}
    \mat A\ones= \ones = 1\cdot\ones, \textnormal{with } \ones \neq \zeros\ ,
\end{equation}
as all rows have unit sum. Thus, $\lambda_1$ is an eigenvalue of all matrices $\mat A$ of our model which are consequentially semi-convergent, if a limit exists.

% if 1 not eigenvalue then convergent
We can summarize these findings in the following \cref{th:semi_conv}.
\begin{theorem}\label{th:semi_conv}
    A squared matrix $\mat A$ is semi-convergent (i.e. $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k \neq \zerosmatr$ exists) if and only if:
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item 1 is an eigenvalue
        \item 1 a semisimple eigenvalue, and
        \item all other eigenvalues have magnitude less than 1. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
As we showed, condition (i) of \cref{th:semi_conv} holds for all row-stochastic matrices. We will discuss under which circumstances the other conditions hold in the following section.

\subsubsection{Reaching a consensus} \label{sec:reaching_consensus}
To now determine when a consensus is reached,  we will utilize the Perron-Frobenius \cref{th:perron-frobenius}, namely irreducible and primitive matrices.
\begin{definition} [Irreducible and primitive matrices] \label{def:irr_prim_mat}
    For $n \geq 2$, a non-negative matrix $\mat A \in \RR_{\geq0}^{n\times n}$ is 
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item \textit{irreducible} if $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mat A^k > 0$
        \item \textit{primitive} if there exists a $k \in \NN$, such that $\mat A^k >0$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}
Moreover, being primitive is a sufficient condition for an irreducible matrix.

If we apply the Perron-Frobenius Theorem to row-stochastic matrices $\mat A$, we get the following.
\begin{theorem}[Perron-Frobenius Theorem]\label{th:perron-frobenius}
    Let the row-stochastic $\mat A \in \{0,1\}^{n\times n}$ with $n\geq 2$, then if $\mat A$ is irreducible,
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ is simple.
    \end{enumerate}
    If additionally $\mat A$ is primitive, then
    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
        \item the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ satisfies $\lambda_1 > |\lambda_i|$ for all other eigenvalues of $\mat A$. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
Therefore, according to the Perron-Frobenius \cref{th:perron-frobenius}, if $\mat A$ is irreducible and row-stochastic, then $\lambda_1$ is simple which is a sufficient condition for semisimple, thus fulfilling condition (ii) of \cref{th:semi_conv}. Moreover, if additionally $\mat A$ is primitive, then all eigenvalues $\lambda_i \neq 1$ have magnitude less than 1, i.e. $\lambda_1 > |\lambda_i|$, thus fulfilling condition (iii) of \cref{th:semi_conv}.
We can therefore conclude that a row-stochastic matrix $\mat A$ is semi-convergent if it is primitive.

However, it can often be computationally expensive to check whether a given matrix is irreducible or primitive. Therefore, we will now relate these conditions to graph properties, which are typically easier to verify.

We can translate any instance of the DeGroot model into a digraph $\gG=(V,E)$ with the nodes $V(\gG) = \{1, \dots,n\}$ representing the individuals and the edges $E(\gG) = \{(i,j)|a_{ij} > 0\}$ with the corresponding weighted adjacency matrix $\mat A$, defined in \cref{sec:degroot}. We can now observe that a path of length $k$ exists from node $i$ to node $j$ in $\gG$ if and only if the entry $(i,j)$ of the k$th$ power of the adjacency matrix $\mat A^k$ is positive.

\begin{proof}
    Let $\mat A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}$. There trivially exists a path of length $1$ from node $i$ to node $j$, precisely when $(A)_{ij}=a_{ij}>0$, i.e. there exists an edge from $i$ to $j$. Assume that the observation is correct for any $k\in \underline{n}$. If there exists a path of length $k+1$ from $i$ to $j$, then there exists a path of length $k$ from $i$ to some node $l\in\underline{n}$ with $(l,j)\in E(\gG)$, i.e. $(\mat A)_{lj}>0$. As $(\mat A^{k+1})_{ij} = (\mat A^k\cdot \mat A)_{ij} = \sum_{m=1}^{n}(\mat A^k)_{im}\cdot (\mat A)_{mj} \geq (\mat A^k)_{il}\cdot (\mat A)_{lj}>0$, because by the induction hypothesis $(\mat A^k)_{il}>0$. In the other direction, if $(\mat A^{k+1})_{ij}>0$ then their must be at least one $l\in \underline{n}$, such that $(\mat A^k)_{il}\cdot (\mat A)_{lj} >0$. Thus, using the induction hypothesis, there must exist a path of length $k$ from $i$ to $l$ and $(l,j)\in E(\gG)$. Therefore, there exists a path of length $k+1$ from $i$ to $j$ over $l$.
\end{proof}

This means that our digraph $\gG$ is strongly connected, if and only if for every pair of nodes $(i,j)$ with $i\neq j$, $(\mat A^k)_{ij}>0$, for a $k<n$. It trivially suffices to ensure this property for $k<n$, as if a path of length $k\geq n$ exists, then at least $n+1$ nodes are visited, i.e. at least one node is visited multiple times. Consequentially, a loop exists in this path which can be removed, resulting in a path of length $k<n$. Thus $\gG$ is strongly connected if and only if $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mat A^k > 0$, i.e. $\mat A$ is irreducible, see \cref{def:irr_prim_mat}. Moreover, as shown in \cite{Bullo_lecture}, the weighted adjacency matrix $\mat A$ is primitive if and only if $\gG$ is strongly connected and aperiodic.\todo{intuition}

\subsubsection{Calculating the consensus}\label{sec:calculating_conv}
However, we are not only interested in the conditions for the model to converge against a consensus, but also in what consensus is reached. Therefore, we will take a look at how the consensus can be calculated, in case it is reached.

% \begin{theorem}\label{th:consensus_value}

As we concluded in \cref{sec:reaching_consensus}, the model converges to a consensus if and only if $\mat A$ is semi-convergent and $\lambda_1$ is simple. Thus, using \cref{eq:col_row_A^k}, $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k = \vec c \cdot\vec r$, where $\vec c = [c_1, \dots, c_n]^\T$ and $\vec r = [r_1, \dots, r_n]$ are the right and left eigenvectors of $\lambda_1$.

If we now choose $\vec c = \ones$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit_A}
    \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k= \ones \vec r = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \vec r\\
        \vdots\\
        \vec r
    \end{bmatrix}_{n\times n} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
         r_1 &  r_2  &\dots &  r_n\\
        \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots\\
         r_1 &  r_2 & \dots &  r_n
    \end{bmatrix}\ .
\end{equation}
To calculate the consensus, we now need to combine this \cref{eq:limit_A} with \cref{eq:iteration}, such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:limit_P}
    \vec{p^{fin}} = \lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\vec p^k = (\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k)\vec p_0 = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \vec r\\
        \vdots\\
        \vec r
    \end{bmatrix}_{n\times n}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        v_1\\
        \vdots\\
        v_n
    \end{bmatrix}\ .
\end{equation}

Therefore, the consensus is equal to 
\[\vec{p_i} = r_1v_1+r_2v_2+\dots +r_nv_n.\]
To summarize, we only require the left eigenvector $\vec{r}$ of $\lambda_1$ of the matrix $\mat{A^{fin}}$, in addition to the vector of the initial opinion values $\vec{p_0}$ to calculate the final opinion values.

\subsubsection{Measuring the speed of convergence}\label{sec:speed_convergence}
Finally, after analyzing when and what consensus is reached, we will examine whether we can measure the speed of convergence, i.e. how long it takes the group to reach a consensus.

Rather intuitively, we can observe that the speed of convergence is dependent on the eigenvalues of matrix $\mat A$. As we have shown in \cref{sec:when_convergence}, the convergence of A can be represented by its Jordan matrix $\mat A^k = \mat T\mat J^k\mat T^{-1}$. The matrix $\mat J^k$ converges when all eigenvalues $|\lambda_i| < 1$, i.e. all eigenvalues except $\lambda = 1$, converge against $0$, with $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_i^k = 0$. Therefore, the speed of convergence is dependent on the size of the eigenvalues of $\mat A$. More specifically, the convergence rate is dependent on the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_{max}$ with $|\lambda_{max}| \geq |\lambda_i|$ for all $i \in \underline{n}$.

% More specifically, if we order the eigenvalue of $\mat A$, with $-1 \leq |\lambda_{m_g}| \leq \dots \leq |\lambda_2| \leq |\lambda_1| = 1$, 
% then the speed is dependent on the size of the second largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$. As argued in \cite{lovasz1993random}, the eigenvalue $\lambda_{m_g}$ is not as relevant, because adding self-loops to each node would result in a positive semi-definite adjacency matrix $\mat A$ and thus in strictly non-negative eigenvalues.

For this $\lambda_{max}$, it trivially holds that $\lambda_{max}^k \geq \lambda_i^k$, with $k > 0$, for all eigenvalues. Therefore, we can define the upper bound 
\[|v_{ik} - \vec r \vec{p_0}| = O(\lambda_{max}^k)\ .\ \cite{DeMarzo}\]
This bound describes that the distance between all opinion values at revision $k$ and the final opinion value is dependent on the size of the eigenvalue $\lambda_{max}$.
Thus, we know for all pairs of semi-convergent, row-stochastic matrices $\mat A$ and $\mat A^{'}$, that if $\lambda_{max} < \lambda_{max}^{'}$, then $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mat A^k$ converges faster than $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(\mat A^{'})^k$. This can be observed in \cref{fig:convergence_speed_quick} and \cref{fig:convergence_speed_low}. The eigenvalue $\lambda_{max}$ of the adjacency matrix used in \cref{fig:convergence_speed_quick} is far smaller than the one of the adjacency matrix used in \cref{fig:convergence_speed_low}. Thus, the model in \cref{fig:convergence_speed_quick} converges quicker asymptotically than the model in \cref{fig:convergence_speed_low}, as can be seen in the figures.

Therefore, we can measure the convergence rate of an instance of the model by calculating the difference between $\lambda_1 - \lambda_{max}$. This eigengap between the dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_{max}$ is also referred to as the spectral gap.

\subsection{Influence of network properties on the convergence}\label{sec:network_prop}
As shown in \cref{sec:reaching_consensus}, we can represent the DeGroot model as a digraph $\gG$ with the individuals as nodes and $\mat A$ as the weighted adjacency matrix. Therefore, we will look at how certain network/graph properties can influence the model.

First, we will look at graphs $\gG$ that consist of multiple strongly connected subgraphs $\gG_i$, i.e. $\gG_i(E) \subset \gG(E)$ and $\gG_i(V) \subset \gG(V)$.
As shown in \cref{sec:reaching_consensus}, the model reaches a consensus if and only if a globally reachable node exists. Thus no consensus is reached for these graphs $\gG$, as such a node cannot exist by definition. However, according to \cite{Bullo_lecture}, the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ is semisimple with the geometric multiplicity $m_g$ and $|\lambda_i| < 1$ for all $i \in \underline{n}$ if and only if $\gG$ has sinks $\gS_i$, see \cref{sec:prelim_notation}, with $i\in\underline{m_g}$, that are all aperiodic. \todo{prüfen ob Satz so gut ist}Such graphs $\gG$, that fulfill these conditions, therefore have semi-convergent adjacency matrices $\mat A$, see \cref{th:semi_conv}.
These graphs $\gG$ reach disagreement, instead of consensus, meaning that at least one pair of final opinion values $v_{i}^{fin} \neq v_{j}^{fin}$, with $i \neq j$.
When calculating the final opinion values, we need to differentiate between strongly connected subgraphs and sinks. As the sinks $\gS_i$ have no outgoing edges, the final opinion values of individuals in the sinks are not dependent on other individuals outside of their sink. Therefore, the final opinion value for nodes $j\in \gS_i$, with $i\in \underline{m_g}$, can be calculated as
\[v_j^{fin} = \vec r_i \vec {p_0},\]
where $\vec{r_i}$ is the corresponding left eigenvector, like in \cref{eq:limit_P}. However, the final opinion values of strongly connected subgraphs that are not sinks, i.e. that have outgoing edges to other strongly connected subgraphs, are dependent on nodes outside of their subgraph. Therefore, the final opinion value for nodes $j\notin \gS_i$, with $i\in \underline{m_g}$, are equal to
\[v_j^{fin} = \sum_{p=1}^{m_g}z_{jp}(\vec{r_i} \vec{p_0})\ ,\]
where $\vec{r_i}$ is the corresponding left eigenvector and $z_{jp}$, with $p\in \underline{m_g}$, are convex combination coefficients that are $z_{jp} > 0$ if and only if there exists a directed path from node $j$ to sink $p$. \cite{Bullo_lecture}

Second, we will discuss how the connectedness of the graph can influence the convergence rate of the model. The conductance $\Phi$ of a graph $\gG$ is a measure to determine how connected a graph is. For a set $S$ of nodes, this measure is defined as
\[\Phi(S) = \frac{\sum_{i\in S,j\notin S}a_{ij}}{min(\sum_{i\in S}\delta(i),\sum_{i\notin S}\delta(i))} = \frac{\sum_{i\in S,j\notin S}a_{ij}}{min(|S|,|V(\gG)\backslash S|)},\]
where $\delta(i)=\sum_{j\in V(\gG)}a_{ij} = 1$. The numerator is the sum of the weights of the edges from $S$ to $V(\gG)\backslash S$, i.e. it describes how much combined weight the nodes in $S$ give to those in $V(\gG)\backslash S$. Therefore, the conductance of a set of nodes $S$ measures the connectedness of $S$ and $V(\gG)\backslash S$ compared to how connected the sets are themselves. For a graph $\gG$, its conductance is defined as $\Phi(\gG) = \min_{S\subset V(\gG)}\Phi(S)$. Thus, if the conductance of a graph $\gG$ is low, there exists a partition of the nodes into two sets ($S$ and $V(\gG)\backslash S)$), such that the nodes $i,j \in S$ or $i,j \notin S$ are highly connected, while nodes $i\in S,\ j\notin  S$ are only sparsely connected. However, if the conductance of the graph $\gG$ is large, then the entirety of $\gG$ is highly connected. As shown in \cite{lovasz1993random}, this measure of conductance can be used to find the upper and lower bounds
\[\frac{\Phi^2}{8}\leq 1-\lambda_2 \leq \Phi,\]
for the eigenvalue $\lambda_2$. These bounds imply that a higher conductance, i.e. connectedness, of the graph, results in a higher spectral gap and therefore smaller eigenvalue $\lambda_2$. Because we observed that $\lambda_2$ is an upper bound for the convergence rate of our model, in \cref{sec:speed_convergence}, we can conclude that highly connected graphs tend to converge faster, than sparsely connected ones.

\section{Extensions of the DeGroot model}\label{sec:extensions}
While the DeGroot model manages to describe the formation of a group consensus, as shown in \cref{sec:degroot}, it consists only of a very simple setup. The only mechanism used to simulate the formation of a consensus is the revision of the opinion value vector $\vec{p_0}$, as shown in \cref{eq:iteration}, which merely takes the initial weights into account, that the individuals assign to one another at the beginning of the process.

As, however, the formation of opinions in real social groups is far more complex than this iterative process, we will look at a few extensions which try to simulate further features of social interaction. 

\subsection{Friedkin-Johnsen model}\label{sec:friedkin-johnsen}
% \subsection{Friedkin-Johnsen model: Stubborn agents}\label{sec:friedkin-johnsen}
The Friedkin-Johnsen model, as introduced in \cite{Friedkin01011990} and later expanded upon in \cite{article}, aims to integrate the phenomenon of interpersonal influences into the DeGroot model. This phenomenon describes the social influence of groups on their members through the encouragement of conformity and discouragement of nonconformity.

The basic idea of the Friedkin-Johnsen model is to simulate the impact of these interpersonal influences by using \textit{susceptibility} as an additional metric. The \textit{susceptibilty} of an individual $i$, denoted as $d_i$, describes how likely this individual is to change their opinion, i.e. the metric $1-d_i$ describes how \textit{stubborn} or unlikely the individual is to change their opinion. A fully stubborn individual $i$ for example, would have $1-d_i = 1$, while a fully susceptible individual $j$ would have $1-d_i= 0$. To use this metric of \textit{susceptibility} in the model, we introduce the matrix

\[ D = \begin{bmatrix}
d_1  &\dots & 0\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
0 & \dots & d_n
\end{bmatrix}\ . \]
The interpersonal influences themselves are then expressed through the matrix $\mat A$, used in the DeGroot model, see \cref{sec:degroot}.

Just like the DeGroot model, the Friedkin-Johnsen model uses an iterative process to revise the opinion values of the individuals of the group. The revised opinion values for the k$th$ revision are calculated as follows.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iteration_friekdin_johnsen}
    \vec{p_k} = \mat D\mat A\vec{p_{k-1}}+(\eye-\mat D)\vec{p_0}
\end{equation}
If the \cref{eq:iteration_friekdin_johnsen} is used iteratively to repeat the revisions, we obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous_iteration_friedkin_johnsen}
    \vec{p_k} = \mat{V_{k-1}}\vec{p_0}\ ,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:helpmatrix_v_friekdin_johnsen}
    \mat V_{k-1} = (\mat D\mat A)^{k-1}+[\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}(\mat D\mat A)^l](\eye-\mat A)\ .
\end{equation}
If the model converges, i.e. $\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\vec{p_k} = \vec{p^{fin}}$ exists, then similar to \cref{eq:convergence}
\begin{multline}
    \vec{p^{fin}} = \mat D \mat A \vec{p^{fin}} + (\eye-\mat D)\vec{p_0} \\\iff (\eye-\mat D \mat A)\vec{p^{fin}} = (\eye-\mat D)\vec{p_0}\ ,
\end{multline}
because when $\vec{p_k}$ converges asymptotically, then $\vec{p_k} = \vec{p_{k-1}}$.
Therefore, if $(\eye-\mat D \mat A)$ is invertible, then
\begin{equation}
    \vec{p^{fin}} = (\eye-\mat D \mat A)^{-1}(\eye-\mat D)\vec{p_0}\ .
\end{equation}

Let us assume that at least one $d_i<1$, otherwise $\mat D = \eye$ and thus \cref{eq:iteration_friekdin_johnsen} is $\vec{p_k} = \mat A\vec{p_{k-1}}$ which is precisely \cref{eq:iteration}, i.e. the model is identical to the DeGroot model. Under this assumption, $(\mat D \mat A)$ is row-substochastic, meaning all row-sums are at most 1 and there is at least one row-sum that is strictly less than 1. According to \cite{Bullo_lecture}, if a row-substochastic matrix $\mat M$ is irreducible then it is also convergent, i.e. $\mat M$ being irreducible is a sufficient condition for the convergence of $\mat M$. Let us now define the following series
\[\mat S_n = \sum_{k=0}^n(\mat D\mat A)^k= \eye + \mat D\mat A + \dots + (\mat D\mat A)^n\ .\]
We can rearrange $(\eye -\mat D\mat A)\mat S_n = (\mat S_n - \mat D\mat A\mat S_n) = (\eye - (\mat D\mat A)^{n+1})$. 
If we assume that $(\mat D\mat A)$ is irreducible and therefore convergent, we know that $\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mat S_n = \mat{S^{fin}}$ exists. Thus, $\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\eye -\mat D\mat A)\mat S_n = (\eye -\mat D\mat A)\mat{S^{fin}}$ is well-defined and $(\eye -\mat D\mat A)\mat{S^{fin}} = \eye$, as $\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\mat D\mat A)^{n+1} = \zerosmatr$. Therefore, we can conclude that $(\eye -\mat D \mat A)$ is invertible if $\mat D\mat A$ is irreducible.

In case $(\eye -\mat D \mat A)$ is not invertible and $\vec{p^{fin}}$ exists, we can calculate $\vec{p^{fin}}$ by iteratively applying \cref{eq:continuous_iteration_friedkin_johnsen} until a sufficiently large $k$ is reached. \cite{article}

In other words, an initially strongly connected graph, representing the group, could be split into multiple strongly connected subgraphs, as discussed in \cref{sec:network_prop}. This effect can be observed in \cref{fig:bounded_conf} which shows the convergence of the model with the adjacency matrix \cref{tb:heatmap_low} used in \cref{fig:convergence_speed_quick}, however with a threshold $m_c = 0.23$. This threshold results in the removal of the edges $(5,2)$ and $(3,6)$. Thus the model is split into two sinks that converge against different final opinion values.

As we ensure that $\mat A$ remains row-stochastic, we need to check whether all strongly connected subgraphs of the corresponding graph $\gG$ are aperiodic. Because this model allows for the removal of edges, we would need to know, what edges will be removed in order to guarantee aperiodicity. Thereby, every node in $\gG$ has a self-loop that cannot be removed, as $m_c >0$, and guaranties that $\gG$ is aperiodic. These self-loops also do not change the final opinion values of the nodes, but only lower the speed of convergence.

People often tend to engage more with others who align closely with their own opinion. Selection and conformation biases are two very well known factors that influence a person's formation of opinions. They both describe how people have a tendency to assign higher weight to information which favors their own opinion. Another effect of this dynamic is that, if an individual $i$ strongly objects to an opinion held by individual $j$, it is likely that $i$ will stop interacting with $j$. The limit for which an opinion can differ for individual $i$ to still engage with that opinion, is referred to as the bounded confidence $\epsilon_i$ of $i$.

To simulate this dynamic, we will take a look at the Hegselmann-Krause model. Unlike the DeGroot model, this model assumes that each individual places an equal weight to all the opinion values of all other individuals that hold similar opinions. Thus, we can define a set of neighbours $ N_i^k = \{1 \leq j \leq n \ |\ |v_{ik} - v_{jk}| < \epsilon_i \}$, that contains all individuals that $i$ interacts with, i.e. that have a sufficiently similar opinion to $i$, in revision $k$.
Therefore, a revision in the Hegselmann-Krause model is calculated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:revision_bounded_conf}
    v_{k+1} = \frac{1}{|N_i^k|}\sum_{j\in N_i^k}v_{jk} \ .
\end{equation}
We can observe that the revised value for a given $v_{ik}$, is the average opinion value of all neighbours of $i$ in revision $k-1$.
Moreover, by redefining our adjacency matrix $\mat A \in \RR_{n\times n}$ as 
\[(\mat A_k)_{ij} = \begin{cases*}
\frac{1}{|N_i^k|} & if $j\in N_i^k$,\\
0 & else.
\end{cases*} \ ,\]
then we can calculate the revision through $\vec{p_k} = \mat{A_k} \vec{p_{k-1}}$, similar to \cref{eq:iteration}.

Notice that this model describes a DeGroot model, where all outgoing edges from a certain node have the same weight and edges between two nodes can be added and removed depending on the simularity of their opinion values.

Furthermore, to observe under which conditions the Hegselmann-Krause model reaches asymptotic consensus, we define an $\epsilon$-chain, similar to \cite{Krause2000DiscreteNonlinearand} and \cite{Dittmer}.

\begin{definition}[$\epsilon$-chain]
    A vector $\vec{p} \in \RR^n_+$ is called an $\epsilon$-chain with $\epsilon \geq 0$ if an ordering $v_{1i} \leq \dots \leq v_{ni}$ exists such that $v_{(k+1)i} - v_{ki} \leq \epsilon$ for all $k \in \underline{n}$.
\end{definition}

With this defintion, we can show in the following that an instance of the Hegselmann-Krause model, with $\epsilon_i = \epsilon$ for all $i\in \underline{n}$, reaches asymptotic consensus if and only if the vector $\vec_{p_k}$ is an $\epsilon$-chain for all revisions k.

\begin{proof}
    Let us assume that $p_k$ is an $\epsilon$-chain for all revisions $k$.
    First, we will prove by induction that the values $v^{max}_k = v_{jk}$ with $v_j = \max_{i \in \underline{n}}v_{i}$ and $v^{min}_k = v_{j'k}$ with $v_{j'} = \min_{i \in \underline{n}}v_{i}$, i.e. the opinion values in revision $k$ of the individuals with the highest and lowest initial opinion values, are an upper and lower bound for all other opinion values with $v^{min}_k \leq v_{ik} \leq v^{max}_k$ for all $i\in \underline{n}$.
    This statement is trivially true for $k=0$, as $v^{max}_0$ and $v^{min}_0$ are precisely defined, as the largest and smallest of the initial opinion values. Assume that the statement is correct for any $k\in \NN$. For any revision $k+1$, let us assume that there exists a $j\in \underline{n}$ such that $v_{j(k+1)} > v^{max}_{k+1}$. Notice that if $i \in N_{max}^{k}$ and $l \in N_j^k$ with $v_{lk} \geq v_{ik}$, then $l\in N_{max}^k$, as $l$ must be in the bounded confidence $\epsilon_{max} = \epsilon$ around $v_k^{max}$. Thus $avg_{i \in N_{max}^k}(v_{ik}) \geq avg_{i \in N_{j}^k}(v_{ik})$, i.e. the average opinion value of the neighbours of $v_k^{max}$ are greater the average opinion value of the neighbours of $v_{jk}$. As we observed for \cref{eq:revision_bounded_conf}, the revised values $v_{k+1}^{max}$ and $v_{j(k+1)}$ are the averages of the opinion values of the neighbours of $max$ and $j$ in revision $k$. Therefore $v^{max}_{k+1} \geq v_{j(k+1)}$. The same argument can be used to show that $v_k^{min}$ is a lower bound. Now, we will show that $v_k^{max}$ and $v_k^{min}$ are strictly increasing and decreasing values. We have shown that $v_k^{max}$ is an upper bound for all opinion values in revision $k$. Because $p_k$ is assumed to be an $\epsilon$-chain, there exists at least one $v_{jk}$ with $j\in N_{max}^k$ and for all $i \in N_{max}^k$, $v_{ik} \leq v_k^{max}$. Thus $v_{k+1}^{max} = \frac{1}{|N_{max}^k|}\sum_{i\in N_{max}^k}v_{ik} \leq v_k^{max}$, i.e. $v_k^{max}$ is a strictly decreasing value. Because $v_k^{max}$ and $v_k^{min}$ are upper and lower bounds as well as strictly decreasing or increasing, it must follow that $p_k$ converges asymptotically for $k\rightarrow\infty$.
\end{proof}

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we examine the DeGroot model to analyze the formation of opinions in groups. We then showed under which conditions the model converges, and more specifically when it converges towards a consensus. Furthermore, we look at how network properties can be used to determine the convergence properties of the model. Lastly, we expand on the DeGroot model examining the effect of additional features, such as \textit{susceptibility}, \textit{confidence bounds}, and \textit{biased agents}, by comparing them to the results of the original model.

In the future, the possible extensions could be further elaborated by taking additional phenomena into account, such as repulsive behaviour ("negative weights" which could change an individual's opinion value in the opposite direction of the one, with whom they interacted), as done in \cite{chen2019opiniondynamicsbackfireeffect}, interrelated topics (a subject rarely exists in isolation but rather in interaction with other topics), as done in \cite{Parsegov_2017}, or a distinction between publicly versus privately expressed opinions (some opinions, especially on highly stigmatized topics, are often expressed only to certain individuals), as done in \cite{Ye_2019}.
